## Identified claim

No third-party SEO platform is 'Google-approved' in the sense many sales pages imply. Google’s new 2026 guidance says it does not evaluate third-party services, those tools do not have internal ranking data, and predictions remain the tool provider’s own.

There is no general class of third-party SEO software that Google Search has certified as **Google-approved**.

Google published new guidance in June 2026 stating that it does not evaluate third-party SEO services.

Google also says third-party tools:

- do not have access to Google’s internal ranking data;
- cannot guarantee performance;
- may provide predictions that do not happen;
- should be evaluated against official Google guidance.

That does not mean SEO tools are useless.

It means the tool’s value comes from what it measures or automates, not from a fictional approval badge.

**Why this search intent matters now.**

The market is crowded with:

- SEO audit tools;
- AI content optimizers;
- GEO platforms;
- AEO tools;
- rank trackers;
- link tools;
- site crawlers;
- schema generators.

Many sales pages imply their recommendations are:

```text
Google compliant
Google approved
what Google wants
based on Google's algorithm
```

Google’s new guidance is effectively a warning label for those claims.

**What third-party tools can do well.**

Useful SEO tools can provide:

- crawl simulation;
- broken-link detection;
- canonical extraction;
- structured-data validation;
- rank tracking;
- backlink discovery;
- log analysis;
- content inventories;
- competitor research;
- change monitoring;
- reporting.

These are observable functions.

You can test whether the tool performs them accurately.

**What they cannot know directly.**

Third-party tools do not have Google’s internal ranking data.

They cannot observe the private weighting of every ranking system.

A tool score such as:

```text
SEO score: 82
Content score: 74
Authority: 53
AI readiness: 91
```

is the vendor’s model.

It is not a Google score unless Google itself produced it.

**Audit recommendations need evidence.**

Suppose a tool says:

> Every article needs 2,000 words.

Google explicitly says it does not have a preferred word count.

The recommendation should be rejected unless there is another valid business or user reason.

Another tool says:

> Put the keyword in exactly 3% of words.

Google warns against keyword stuffing.

Again, the tool is not the authority.

**GEO tools deserve the same skepticism.**

Google’s June guidance specifically mentions AEO and GEO tools.

A tool can legitimately measure:

- observed AI mentions;
- citations;
- prompt sets;
- competitor presence.

It cannot guarantee that adding an “AI score” from 61 to 88 will cause Google AI Mode to cite the page.

Ask the vendor to distinguish:

```text
observation
prediction
recommendation
Google documentation
opinion
```

Those categories should not be blended.

**Search Console is first-party.**

Google strongly encourages site owners to use Search Console for data directly from Google Search.

That makes Search Console the best source for questions such as:

- did Google index this URL?
- what queries generated impressions?
- which page received clicks?
- what canonical did Google select?
- did Search Console report a manual action?

Third-party tools can enrich that data.

They should not overwrite it without explanation.

**How to evaluate an SEO audit platform.**

**1. Export findings.**

Can you export the issue list?

A tool that only shows a score is hard to audit.

**2. Inspect the evidence.**

For each recommendation, ask:

```text
URL
observed state
why it matters
official source
severity
confidence
```

**3. Check false positives.**

Sample 50 findings.

How many are actually problems?

**4. Separate preference from defect.**

Example:

```text
Title is 62 characters
```

is not automatically an SEO error.

Google does not impose one universal title-character limit.

**5. Require change safety.**

A tool should not automatically rewrite:

- canonicals;
- robots;
- redirects;
- structured data;
- titles;

across a production site without review.

**Red flags in vendor language.**

Be cautious around:

- “approved by Google”;
- “inside Google ranking data”;
- “guaranteed #1”;
- “secret AI ranking factor”;
- “AI Mode schema”;
- “we submit you to thousands of search engines”;
- “Google partnership means organic rankings.”

Google’s hiring-an-SEO guidance specifically warns about guaranteed rankings, link schemes, and unsolicited SEO sales.

**AI-generated audit reports.**

AI can summarize crawl data usefully.

But a generated recommendation still needs evidence.

Require:

```text
source observation
rule
confidence
supporting documentation
human approval
```

Do not let a language model turn one warning into 5,000 automated changes.

**Buyer scorecard.**

Evaluate tools on:

- crawl accuracy;
- data provenance;
- exportability;
- API;
- change history;
- false-positive rate;
- documentation;
- privacy;
- role access;
- cancellation;
- pricing;
- support.

Not:

> The dashboard feels very green.

## Conclusion

**FAQ.**

**Does Google approve SEO tools?**

Google says it does not evaluate third-party SEO services.

**Do SEO tools have Google ranking data?**

Not Google’s private internal ranking data. Some tools can use Search Console data when you authorize access.

**Are GEO tools fake?**

Not automatically. They can provide useful observational data. Their recommendations and predictions remain vendor-defined.

**Is Search Console enough?**

It is the key first-party Google data source, but specialized tools can add useful crawl, link, monitoring, and workflow capabilities.

**Can any tool guarantee ranking improvement?**

Google says third-party tools cannot guarantee performance.

**Vendor checklist.**

- Data source documented.
- Google data separated from proprietary metrics.
- Recommendations cite evidence.
- False positives sampled.
- Export available.
- API permissions scoped.
- Automated changes reviewable.
- GEO observations reproducible.
- Predictions labeled.
- No “Google-approved” implication.
- Contract and cancellation reviewed.

**Verdict.**

Buy SEO software for the work it demonstrably performs.

Do not buy it because the marketing page borrowed Google’s vocabulary and placed a sparkle icon next to a proprietary score.

Google’s new guidance gives buyers permission to ask the obvious question: **show me the evidence.**

**Conclusion in brief.**

No third-party SEO platform is 'Google-approved' in the sense many sales pages imply. Google’s new 2026 guidance says it does not evaluate third-party services, those tools do not have internal ranking data, and predictions remain the tool provider’s own.

## Limitations

The cited documents establish only the scoped product and control behavior they describe. They do not expose ranking weights, guarantee crawler timing, prove results for every site, or establish that third-party observations are universal. Product availability and interface behavior can vary by location, account, rollout, configuration, and time. Recheck the cited primary documentation before changing crawl, index, privacy, training, or vendor policies, and validate changes in the affected environment. A missing observation is not proof that a feature never operates, while one observed result is not proof that it always does.
