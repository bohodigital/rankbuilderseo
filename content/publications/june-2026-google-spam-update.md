## Checklist

Google’s June 2026 spam update was global, applied to all languages, and finished in roughly two days. Google did not announce one special target. Recovery therefore starts by auditing actual spam-policy exposure instead of copying whatever theory happens to be loudest on social media.

Google’s **June 2026 spam update** started June 24 and completed June 26, 2026.[@rb-handoff-20-04-source-1]

Google said the update:

- applied globally;
- applied to all languages;
- was a normal spam update.

Google did **not** publicly say:

- it only targeted AI content;
- it only targeted links;
- it only targeted affiliates;
- it only targeted parasite SEO;
- it only targeted expired domains;
- it only targeted one CMS.

If your site fell, audit the actual spam policies.

Do not retrofit a rumor into a diagnosis.

Use these checks as the working list:

- Why this query is hot
- map the timeline
- understand what “spam” means
- audit scaled content abuse
- audit site reputation abuse
- audit link acquisition
- audit cloaking and redirects
- audit keyword stuffing
- audit thin affiliate content
- check manual actions
- stop trying to manipulate AI responses
- Recovery checklist

**Why this query is hot.**

The June spam update is currently the latest ranking incident on Google’s public status dashboard.[@rb-handoff-20-04-source-1]

It arrived only weeks after the May 2026 core update.

That creates a messy overlap for any site that changed between late May and late June.

Your first task is to separate:

```text
core update
spam update
technical incident
seasonality
SERP change
site change
```

**Step 1: map the timeline.**

Record:

```text
May 21: May core update begins
June 2: May core update ends
June 24: June spam update begins
June 26: June spam update ends
```

Then annotate your own changes:

- migration;
- redesign;
- CDN;
- internal linking;
- content launch;
- link campaign;
- indexation setting;
- robots change;
- template release.

A ranking loss on June 11 is difficult to blame on an update that began June 24.

**Step 2: understand what “spam” means.**

Google defines spam as practices used to deceive users or manipulate Search systems, including attempts to manipulate generative AI responses in Google Search.[@rb-handoff-20-04-source-2]

The policies cover many behaviors.

Relevant categories include:

- cloaking;
- doorway abuse;
- expired domain abuse;
- hacked content;
- hidden text and links;
- keyword stuffing;
- link spam;
- machine-generated traffic;
- malicious practices;
- misleading functionality;
- scaled content abuse;
- scraping;
- site reputation abuse;
- sneaky redirects;
- thin affiliation;
- user-generated spam.

A site can violate more than one policy.

**Step 3: audit scaled content abuse.**

This is the obvious 2026 risk.

Google defines scaled content abuse as generating many pages primarily to manipulate rankings while providing little value, regardless of whether the pages were made by AI, humans, or a combination.[@rb-handoff-20-04-source-2]

Red flags:

- thousands of near-identical pages;
- scraped definitions;
- stitched summaries;
- auto-translated pages with no local value;
- generated pages for every keyword permutation;
- mass-produced comparison pages with no research;
- city pages that change only the city name.

AI is not the policy.

Scale plus low value plus ranking manipulation is the problem.

**Step 4: audit site reputation abuse.**

Ask whether third-party content is exploiting the host site’s existing signals.

Examples can include third-party sections whose primary purpose is ranking because they live on a strong domain.

Review:

- coupon folders;
- white-label reviews;
- gambling sections;
- third-party loan pages;
- sponsored directories;
- unrelated affiliate hubs.

Editorial third-party content is not automatically abuse.

Purpose and oversight matter.

**Step 5: audit link acquisition.**

Look for:

- paid followed links;
- link exchanges at scale;
- private blog networks;
- automated guest posts;
- expired domains redirected for authority;
- widget links;
- sitewide footer links;
- low-quality directories;
- hacked links.

Do not panic-delete every backlink.

Identify links your organization created, bought, exchanged, or controlled.

**Step 6: audit cloaking and redirects.**

Compare:

```text
Googlebot
normal browser
logged-out user
mobile
desktop
different countries
```

Look for:

- different text;
- hidden keyword blocks;
- bot-specific redirects;
- hacked injections;
- security software showing another page;
- affiliate interstitials.

Use rendered HTML, server logs, and curl.

**Step 7: audit keyword stuffing.**

Google still explicitly prohibits unnatural repetition of terms.[@rb-handoff-20-04-source-2]

Watch:

```text
SEO company Chicago
best SEO company Chicago
Chicago SEO company
affordable Chicago SEO company
top Chicago SEO company
```

repeated in one paragraph like language has been taken hostage.

Use the words people search for.

Do not use all of them every four sentences.

**Step 8: audit thin affiliate content.**

Google’s current policy distinguishes useful affiliate pages from thin affiliation.[@rb-handoff-20-04-source-2]

Useful:

- original testing;
- price comparison;
- data;
- unique reviews;
- decision tools;
- photos;
- pros and cons;
- meaningful comparisons.

Thin:

- merchant descriptions copied across hundreds of pages;
- no original evaluation;
- affiliate link as the page’s only purpose.

**Step 9: check manual actions.**

Automated spam systems and manual actions are different.

Open Search Console:

```text
Security & Manual Actions
→ Manual actions
```

If a manual action exists, the report identifies the issue.

Fix the violation comprehensively and submit a reconsideration request when appropriate.

If no manual action exists, there is no reconsideration request for an algorithmic ranking loss.

**Step 10: stop trying to manipulate AI responses.**

Google’s 2026 spam-policy language explicitly includes attempts to manipulate generative AI responses.[@rb-handoff-20-04-source-2]

Do not create:

- thousands of “fan-out” pages;
- hidden prompt text;
- fake citations;
- AI-targeted keyword blocks;
- fictional experts;
- fake firsthand experience.

Google’s AI optimization guide specifically warns against scaled pages for possible fan-out queries.[@rb-handoff-20-04-source-3]

**Recovery checklist.**

- Update dates compared correctly.
- Manual Actions checked.
- Security Issues checked.
- Scaled pages inventoried.
- Site reputation sections reviewed.
- Paid and exchanged links reviewed.
- Redirects tested.
- Googlebot parity tested.
- Keyword stuffing reviewed.
- Affiliate value reviewed.
- Hacked content ruled out.
- UGC spam ruled out.
- AI-targeted manipulation removed.
- Valuable pages preserved.
- Changes documented.

## Completion criteria

**FAQ.**

**What did the June 2026 spam update target?**

Google did not announce a single specific target. It described it as a normal global spam update.

**Did it target AI content?**

Google did not say the June update specifically targeted AI content. Google’s broader policy can penalize scaled low-value content regardless of how it was produced.

**How long did the update run?**

About two days, from June 24 through June 26.

**Can I submit reconsideration?**

Only when Search Console shows a manual action and you have fixed it. Algorithmic changes do not have a generic reconsideration form.

**Should I remove all affiliate links?**

No. Google explicitly says legitimate affiliate content can be useful when it adds meaningful value.

**Verdict.**

The June 2026 spam update does not need a conspiracy theory.

Google published a long list of prohibited patterns.

Audit the patterns you actually use, remove manipulation, preserve genuinely useful content, and keep a change log so the next ranking movement can be interpreted instead of guessed.

The audit is complete only when the relevant pages, scripts, tags, redirects, vendors, and Search Console records have been checked and the findings are documented. This checklist reflects Google documentation available through 2026-08-06. Policies, enforcement systems, and interfaces can change, and passing a checklist does not guarantee rankings or traffic.
