## Identified claim

Premium Home Service is the subject of a pending federal and Illinois lawsuit alleging thousands of fabricated local business listings, made-up addresses, fake five-star reviews, misleading dispatch promises, and misuse of consumer financial information.

Premium Home Service is a real operating entity named in a serious pending government lawsuit.

The current public record does not support calling the allegations a final court finding. It does support a strong warning:

> Do not rely on a Premium Home Service listing, address, review profile, or local identity without independent verification.

In May 2026, the U.S. Department of Justice, acting for the FTC, and the Illinois Attorney General sued B.E.S.T. GDR LLC, which does business as Premium Home Service, and its owner.

The complaint alleges that the operation created thousands of fake online business profiles for home-repair companies and used fabricated reviews to divert people searching for local electricians, plumbers, HVAC providers, and garage-door services.

The case was pending at the time of this audit.

**Why this belongs on an SEO site.**

Premium Home Service is not a conventional SEO agency.

It is relevant because the alleged conduct is a direct abuse of local search:

- keyword-rich business names;
- fabricated local addresses;
- local phone numbers;
- fake reviews;
- rating manipulation;
- lead routing;
- geographic misrepresentation;
- search-result capture.

This is what local SEO looks like after every ethical and factual constraint has been thrown into a wood chipper.

**What the government alleges.**

The government complaint alleges that Premium Home Service operated through scores of fabricated business names around the country.

According to the FTC’s summary, many profiles used addresses belonging to unrelated third parties or addresses that were made up.

The listings allegedly presented the businesses as established local operators.

Consumers calling those profiles were allegedly routed to representatives elsewhere, including overseas call centers.

The complaint further alleges that consumers were told a technician could arrive within a specified window even when representatives did not know whether one was available.

**The review allegations.**

The government alleges that the defendants posted fabricated five-star reviews.

Those reviews allegedly appeared to come from customers of the fake local companies.

The FTC says the positive reviews were also used to dilute genuine one-star reviews and improve the apparent rating.

That distinction matters.

This is not merely an accusation of selectively requesting reviews from happy customers. The complaint describes reviews that were allegedly created, bought, or supplied by employees and relatives and associated with fabricated local entities.

The FTC tied the alleged conduct to its Reviews and Testimonials Rule.

**The local-address problem.**

A local address is not decorative metadata.

It tells a consumer:

- where the company operates;
- which laws and licenses may apply;
- whether a storefront exists;
- whether the company knows the area;
- who can be located after a dispute.

A made-up or borrowed address can create an appearance of proximity while making accountability harder.

Before hiring any home-service provider found through local search:

1. Search the legal entity in state records.
2. Confirm the address through the company’s own site and public records.
3. Verify trade licensing.
4. Ask for the technician’s company and license.
5. Request a written estimate.
6. Confirm who processes payment.
7. Do not rely on the business-profile name alone.

**Technician and quality allegations.**

The complaint alleges that in some cases no technician arrived.

In other cases, Premium Home Service allegedly arranged for technicians who were not qualified or licensed for the work, leading to substandard or dangerous results.

Those are government allegations, not findings in this article.

The buyer lesson is independent of the litigation:

> A call center’s promise is not proof that a qualified local contractor has accepted the job.

Ask for the dispatched company’s identity before authorizing work.

**Financial-information allegation.**

The complaint also alleges violations of the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act based on false or fraudulent statements used to obtain consumers’ financial information.

That elevates the issue beyond ordinary disappointing service.

Do not provide card or bank information until the legal merchant, scope, price, and cancellation terms are clear.

## Conclusion

**Is the company a scam?**

The legally accurate answer is:

- a federal and state complaint alleges fraudulent and deceptive conduct;
- the case was pending on the audit date;
- the allegations have not been reduced to a final judgment in the sources reviewed;
- the described local-search behavior is serious enough to justify avoidance while the case proceeds.

Calling every pending defendant conclusively guilty is reckless.

Ignoring the complaint because it is pending is equally stupid.

**How businesses can defend their local market.**

Legitimate local companies should monitor:

- Google Business Profile competitors;
- duplicate names;
- fake addresses;
- copied photos;
- fabricated review patterns;
- tracking numbers;
- sudden ranking displacement;
- unlicensed operators.

Preserve screenshots, listing URLs, review text, dates, and call evidence.

Use platform reporting channels and report suspected fraud to appropriate regulators or law enforcement.

Do not launch retaliatory review campaigns.

**Eight questions for any local-service listing.**

1. What is the legal company name?
2. What state is it registered in?
3. What is the physical business address?
4. Who is the licensed contractor?
5. Is the person answering the phone an employee or lead broker?
6. Who will appear on the card charge?
7. What written warranty applies?
8. Where can a complaint or lawsuit be served?

**Verdict.**

Premium Home Service should be treated as a high-risk provider while the FTC, DOJ, and Illinois case remains pending.

The public record supports avoiding reliance on its local profiles and independently verifying every entity, address, technician, review, and payment recipient.

The article does not convert allegations into findings. It explains why the allegations target the structural foundations of local search trust.
