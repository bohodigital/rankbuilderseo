## Identified claim

Publishing.com is a real self-publishing education business under a July 2026 final FTC order. The agency alleged misleading income claims, difficult refund conditions, undisclosed connected reviewers, and incentivized positive testimonials.

Publishing.com is a real operating education and self-publishing business.

In July 2026, the FTC finalized an order with Publishing.com and two principals.

The order required $1.5 million and imposed restrictions concerning:

- earnings claims;
- material misrepresentations;
- cancellation and refund terms;
- review and testimonial representations;
- undisclosed material connections;
- incentivized reviews.

The company was formerly known as PublishingLife.com.

A buyer should not treat a real company, polished training portal, or successful founder story as proof that the typical purchaser will earn similar income.

## Sources and evidence

**Sources reviewed.**

1. [FTC final Publishing.com order announcement](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/07/ftc-approves-final-order-against-publishingcom-settling-allegations-it-misled-consumers) — Federal Trade Commission; accessed 2026-08-05. [@rb-company-risk-08-source-1]
2. [Publishing.com case record](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/242-3055-publishingcom-matter) — Federal Trade Commission; accessed 2026-08-05. [@rb-company-risk-08-source-2]

**What Publishing.com sold.**

Publishing.com marketed programs and services intended to help consumers earn money publishing e-books and audiobooks.

The FTC alleged that advertising represented substantial income opportunities.

The agency said most buyers did not earn the income promised in the advertising.

That gap is central.

A business-opportunity seller cannot substantiate a broad earnings impression with a handful of exceptional case studies.

**Founder stories and typical results.**

A founder may have earned substantial income.

That does not establish what a new buyer is likely to earn.

A useful earnings disclosure should explain:

- number of purchasers;
- number who attempted the method;
- median revenue;
- median profit;
- time period;
- advertising cost;
- outsourcing cost;
- platform fees;
- refunds;
- taxes;
- failure rate;
- selection criteria.

“Some students made six figures” answers almost none of those questions.

**Refund conditions.**

The FTC alleged that consumers seeking refunds encountered additional conditions that were buried in fine print or lengthy terms.

A refund promise is part of the offer.

Before buying, capture:

- refund window;
- required milestones;
- coaching attendance;
- content-completion threshold;
- proof required;
- submission method;
- response deadline;
- exclusions;
- chargeback clause;
- arbitration;
- governing law.

Do not rely on a sales-call description that is absent from the written contract.

**Review conflicts.**

The FTC alleged that Publishing.com failed to disclose reviews written by employees and other connected people, including relatives of the principals.

The agency also alleged that incentives were offered for positive testimonials.

A connected person can give an honest opinion.

The problem is presenting that opinion as independent.

A testimonial should disclose:

- employee relationship;
- family relationship;
- affiliate commission;
- free access;
- discount;
- contest entry;
- coaching relationship;
- paid appearance.

**The final order.**

The final order prohibits misleading earnings claims and requires a reasonable basis for future claims.

It also requires clear treatment of cancellation, refunds, endorsements, and material connections.

This is current, primary-source evidence.

It is not an anonymous online accusation.

## Conclusion

**Is Publishing.com a scam?**

The evidence supports a more exact conclusion.

Publishing.com is a real company under a final FTC order resolving serious allegations.

The company is not a vendor a cautious buyer should purchase from based on sales testimonials or earnings claims alone.

A buyer should proceed only after reviewing:

- current order compliance;
- written refund policy;
- typical net results;
- complete cost;
- content ownership;
- platform dependencies;
- independent reviews;
- cancellation process.

**Self-publishing business reality.**

Publishing can produce income.

It also requires:

- market research;
- editing;
- design;
- production;
- rights clearance;
- platform compliance;
- advertising;
- audience development;
- royalty accounting;
- repeated releases.

The existence of a working business model does not validate every training offer built around it.

Selling shovels near a gold field remains selling shovels.

**Questions to ask.**

1. What percentage of all buyers earns a profit?
2. Is the figure revenue or net profit?
3. What is the median result?
4. Which costs are excluded?
5. How many buyers requested refunds?
6. How many refunds were approved?
7. Who wrote the featured testimonials?
8. Were reviewers compensated?
9. What intellectual property does the buyer own?
10. What happens if Amazon or another platform suspends the account?

**Verdict.**

Publishing.com is legitimate as a real business, but its final FTC order is a major buyer warning.

No one should buy the program based on income imagery, founder success, positive testimonials, or a verbal refund promise.

The decision belongs to audited numbers and written terms.

**Conclusion in brief.**

Publishing.com is a real self-publishing education business under a July 2026 final FTC order. The agency alleged misleading income claims, difficult refund conditions, undisclosed connected reviewers, and incentivized positive testimonials.
