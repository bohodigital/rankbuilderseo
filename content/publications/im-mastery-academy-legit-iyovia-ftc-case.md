## Identified claim

IM Mastery Academy has operated under names including iMarketsLive, IM Academy, and IYOVIA. The FTC and Nevada alleged that the investment-training and multi-level-marketing operation used false earnings claims and took in more than $1.2 billion.

IM Mastery Academy is a real training and multi-level-marketing operation with an exceptionally serious government record.

The Federal Trade Commission and the State of Nevada sued the operation in May 2025.

The complaint alleged that the scheme took in more than $1.2 billion while using false or baseless earnings claims to sell financial-market training and recruit people into a multi-level-marketing venture.

The public record connects the operation with several names:

- iMarketsLive;
- IM Mastery Academy;
- IM Academy;
- IYOVIA;
- International Markets Live.

A federal court issued a preliminary injunction in August 2025. Several defendants entered settlements in 2025, and the FTC announced major settlements with lead defendants in May 2026. The case page still listed the broader matter as pending on the audit date.

**What the company sold.**

The operation sold training related to financial markets and an MLM business opportunity in which participants could market the training to others.

Those are separate value propositions:

```text
Education value:
Can the course teach useful and accurate skills?

Business-opportunity value:
Can a typical participant earn a net profit selling access to the course?
```

A program can contain real educational material while the associated earnings opportunity remains misleading.

Do not let one working trading tool validate an entire recruitment model.

**The earnings allegations.**

The FTC and Nevada alleged that defendants used false or baseless earnings claims.

Consumers were reportedly shown images and messages suggesting substantial income from trading or the MLM opportunity.

A meaningful earnings disclosure needs:

- all participants;
- active participants;
- median gross commissions;
- median net profit;
- subscription costs;
- event costs;
- marketing expenses;
- refund rate;
- dropout rate;
- percentage earning zero;
- time period.

A top-rank earner’s result does not describe the ordinary recruit.

**Trading education is not investment performance.**

A course can explain:

- chart patterns;
- risk management;
- foreign exchange;
- cryptocurrency;
- options;
- market psychology;
- technical indicators.

That does not mean the student will profit.

Before paying for trading education, ask for:

- instructor qualifications;
- conflicts of interest;
- audited performance claims;
- risk disclosures;
- simulated-versus-live results;
- drawdowns;
- fees;
- product suitability;
- regulator registration where applicable.

A screenshot of one profitable trade is not a track record.

**The MLM structure.**

In an MLM, compensation can depend on retail sales, recruitment, team volume, rank, or a mixture.

The buyer needs to determine:

- whether retail demand exists outside participants;
- what percentage of revenue comes from members;
- how many recruits earn more than expenses;
- whether product purchases are required;
- whether events and subscriptions are effectively mandatory;
- whether rank maintenance depends on recruiting.

The FTC’s complaint alleged that the operation used similar earnings claims to sell both training and the MLM venture.

**Marketing to young people.**

The FTC and Nevada alleged deliberate marketing to young people, including posts directed at college social-media pages.

Young recruits can be particularly vulnerable to:

- financial-inexperience gaps;
- social pressure;
- aspirational lifestyle marketing;
- credit-card debt;
- peer recruitment;
- fear of missing out;
- confusion between trading and entrepreneurship.

A campus-oriented pitch should receive more scrutiny, not less.

A buyer should review whether the current offer is bound by an existing court order.

**The 2025 and 2026 orders.**

The FTC announced several settlements:

- a $2.5 million settlement with three defendants in August 2025;
- additional settlements involving $10.5 million in September 2025;
- settlements with lead defendants announced in May 2026 involving tens of millions of dollars in assets;
- entered orders listed on the case timeline through July 2026.

The exact obligations differ by defendant.

Do not compress the entire case into one monetary figure or imply every individual agreed to the same terms.

## Conclusion

**Is IM Mastery Academy a scam?**

The FTC and Nevada publicly characterized the alleged operation as a wide-ranging scam.

That characterization is tied to the government complaint and must be attributed.

The combination of:

- more than $1.2 billion in alleged consumer spending;
- false earnings allegations;
- MLM recruitment;
- youth targeting allegations;
- preliminary injunction;
- multiple settlements;
- major asset turnover;

supports a strong avoid recommendation for new buyers and recruits.

**Questions for a current recruiter.**

1. Which legal entity sells the current subscription?
2. Which FTC order applies?
3. What percentage of all participants earns a net profit?
4. What is the median result after fees?
5. How much revenue comes from nonparticipant retail customers?
6. Are trading results live, audited, and risk-adjusted?
7. Which instructors hold relevant credentials?
8. What expenses are required to maintain rank?
9. Can the subscription be canceled online?
10. What happens to the downline after the distributor leaves?

**Verdict.**

Avoid IM Mastery Academy, IYOVIA, IM Academy, iMarketsLive, and related opportunity pitches tied to the covered operation.

The public record is not a handful of angry comments. It includes a government complaint, preliminary injunction, entered orders, multiple settlements, and tens of millions of dollars in announced asset turnover.

Anyone considering the educational product separately should evaluate it as education, without assuming that trading profitability or MLM income follows from access to the course.
