## Definition

Keyword density is the percentage of words on a page represented by a particular keyword or phrase. If a 1,000-word article uses an exact phrase 20 times, a simple density calculation might call that roughly 2 percent. SEO tools sometimes report this number because it is easy to calculate. The problem begins when the number is treated as a target Google expects.

Google's current SEO Starter Guide tells site owners not to focus on outdated or overemphasized ideas and specifically warns against keyword stuffing. [@kd-starter-guide] Search Essentials takes a more useful approach: use words that people would use to look for your content and put those words in prominent places such as the title, main heading, alt text, and link text. [@kd-search-essentials] That is guidance about descriptive language and context, not a prescribed density formula.

The distinction matters because keyword density converts writing into arithmetic while search systems evaluate much more than arithmetic.

## Mechanism

Search engines need language signals to understand a page. A page about emergency plumbing should probably contain ordinary language associated with emergency plumbing. Hiding the phrase entirely because you fear optimization would be as silly as repeating it every other sentence because a plugin wants a green light.

Google's Search Essentials explicitly recommends using the words searchers use in prominent locations. [@kd-search-essentials] That supports deliberate keyword research and clear topical language. But Google's spam policies separately define keyword stuffing as filling a page with keywords or numbers in an attempt to manipulate rankings, including unnatural repetition of the same words or phrases. [@kd-spam-policy]

There is no contradiction. Relevance requires language. Manipulation begins when repetition stops serving the reader and starts serving a presumed formula.

A density percentage also ignores where a term appears. One clear use in the title, a descriptive main heading, a concise opening answer, and natural mentions where the concept is discussed can communicate more than ten awkward repetitions buried in boilerplate. It also ignores synonyms, entities, supporting concepts, examples, and the broader meaning of the page.

Google's current starter guidance emphasizes creating useful content and explicitly discourages obsessing over tactics that do not meaningfully improve the page. [@kd-starter-guide] That is a better mental model than asking whether 1.5 percent or 2.3 percent is the secret number.

## Examples

Imagine a page targeting the query `how to fix a leaking faucet`. A useful page might use that phrase or close variants in the title, opening answer, a heading, and naturally throughout the instructions. It will also mention washers, cartridges, shutoff valves, handles, water supply, tools, and the symptoms that distinguish different failures. The page becomes relevant because it answers the problem comprehensively, not because the exact phrase appears 17 times.

Now imagine an editor forcing the exact phrase into every paragraph to reach a 3 percent density target. Sentences begin sounding mechanical: fix a leaking faucet quickly, leaking faucet repair guide, best way to fix a leaking faucet. That repetition adds no information. It can make the page unpleasant to read and, at the extreme, resemble the kind of keyword stuffing Google lists in its spam policy. [@kd-spam-policy]

A local-service page provides another example. It is reasonable to say what service you offer and where you offer it. It is not reasonable to paste dozens of city names or nearly identical location phrases into blocks whose only purpose is to create ranking signals. Google specifically gives blocks of cities and regions as an example of keyword stuffing when they are used without substantial added value. [@kd-spam-policy]

For content teams, a better workflow is query-first rather than density-first. Identify the main search question. Write the clearest direct answer. Cover the subquestions a competent reader would need answered. Use the primary phrase where it accurately labels the subject. Then edit for clarity, duplication, and missing concepts.

Keyword counts can still be diagnostic. If an important term appears zero times, perhaps the article is strangely indirect. If it appears 80 times in a short page, perhaps the copy is overoptimized. The count is a flag for human review, not a score to maximize.

## Boundaries

Keyword density is not useless as a descriptive statistic. Editors can use it to notice extreme repetition, compare drafts, or investigate why generated copy sounds unnatural. The problem is treating the statistic as a ranking requirement.

This article also should not be misread as advice to avoid keywords. Google tells site owners to use the words people search for and place them in important descriptive locations. [@kd-search-essentials] Search-friendly writing is often more explicit than literary writing because ambiguity helps neither users nor crawlers.

The practical standard is semantic usefulness. Does the phrase identify the page accurately? Does it help the reader understand a heading, link, image, or answer? Would you keep the wording if a keyword-density meter disappeared tomorrow? If yes, the usage is probably defensible.

A good SEO page therefore has enough keyword use to be unmistakably about its subject and enough editorial restraint to avoid sounding like it was written for a counter. There is no magic percentage hiding between those two goals.
